Why joints degrade — and what slows it

Osteoarthritis in dogs is a slow, cumulative process. Cartilage thins, synovial fluid loses viscosity, and low-grade inflammation settles in. It's estimated that up to 20% of dogs over one year old have some degree of OA, and the number climbs steeply in senior years. Supplements can't rebuild cartilage — but decent evidence suggests several can slow the breakdown, ease discomfort, and improve mobility.

The trap is dose. Many brands list ingredients but bury the quantities in proprietary blends, making it impossible to know if your dog is getting a research-supported amount. Our first filter for every product below: the dose has to be on the label.

Talk to your vet first. If your dog shows a sudden change in mobility — not a gradual one — that's a veterinary appointment, not a supplement order. Rule out injury, neurological issues, or worse before you shop.

Glucosamine, chondroitin & the rest: what the evidence says

Here's the honest short version of the research landscape:

  • Glucosamine sulfate / hydrochloride — the most-studied joint ingredient in dogs, usually paired with chondroitin. Results are mixed but generally positive; it works best early and slowly.
  • Chondroitin sulfate — often included alongside glucosamine; evidence is thin but it's low-risk and cheap to add.
  • Green-lipped mussel (GLM) — the quiet standout. A 2020 randomized trial found significant improvement in lameness and pain scores in dogs with hip OA. We weight it heavily.
  • Omega-3 fatty acids (EPA/DHA) — genuinely good anti-inflammatory support for joints; also a nice bonus for skin and kidneys.
  • CBD — promising anecdotes, but clinical evidence in dogs remains early and dosing is guesswork. We don't rank it; we flag it as "talk to your vet."

How to choose for your dog

A good joint supplement is chosen with three things in mind: your dog's body weight (dosing scales with it), their current mobility level, and your routine. Our decision checklist:

  1. Check the dose is printed. No label amounts, no proprietary blends — skip it.
  2. Prefer multicomponent formulas. Glucosamine + chondroitin + GLM or omega-3 covers more ground than any single ingredient.
  3. Look for third-party testing. NASC logo or an independently published certificate of analysis is worth a premium.
  4. Give it an honest 6–8 weeks. Joint supplements are slow-acting. Judge on stairs, couch behavior, and walk length — not day three enthusiasm.
Stick with soft chews your dog will actually take. The best formula in the world does nothing sitting uneaten under the fridge. Cosequin-style tablets are fine too if your dog swallows pills happily.

The part supplements can't do

Supplements quietly help, but they can't out-consult your vet, out-exercise a sedentary dog, or out-lift extra weight. Weight management remains the single best thing you can do for arthritic joints. For many senior dogs, a simple anti-inflammatory protocol from your vet — plus a good supplement, plenty of gentle movement, and a warm, padded bed — is the difference between "managing" and "thriving."

Frequently asked questions

Short answers to the questions we get most — the longer reasoning is above.

How long before I see a difference?

Most published trials run 6–12 weeks, and owners typically report the clearest change inside that window. Watch for small things: less stiffness first thing in the morning, an easier time on the stairs, a longer first walk of the day.

Can my dog take a joint supplement alongside other medications?

Usually yes — but it should always be confirmed. NSAIDs, certain blood thinners, and prescription diets can interact with supplement ingredients, and senior pets are often on multiple things at once. Run any new supplement past your vet first.

Are chews or tablets better?

Neither is more effective — acceptance is the difference. Soft chews are easier for most senior dogs to take and split into half doses; tablets are the better value if your dog swallows them happily. Pick whichever your dog will actually consume consistently.

Do these work for cats too?

Glucosamine and chondroitin are used in cats, but dosing differs dramatically by body weight — and cats with kidney disease need vet-supervised choices, since some ingredients are contraindicated. Chat with your vet before adding a joint supplement to a cat's routine.
